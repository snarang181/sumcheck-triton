# zkDuel on H100: findings

H100 PCIe reproduction of the paper's 800-configuration zkPHIRE sweep, with the paper's
Table 5 software stack (see `README.md` for the environment and its deviations), plus
follow-up measurements that test the paper's explanation. Numbers below come from
`FINDINGS_TABLES.md` (generated by `tools/make_findings_tables.py` from the recorded data)
unless another file is named.

**Platforms.** The published paper (HASP '26) uses this H100 PCIe as its primary platform and
the A100 as a portability platform. This analysis was written against the submitted version of
the paper, whose numbers come from the A100-SXM4 sweep in `../a100/`: "the paper" below means that
version, and "this run" means the first H100 sweep in `sweep/`. The published paper's H100 sweep
numbers (Tables 2 and 4, Figure 4) come from the fixed-harness sweep in `sweep_fixed/`
(`run_fixed_sweep.sh`), which is not tabulated here; `tools/summarize_sweep.py` summarizes
it with the definitions used below.

## 1. Summary

1. **The paper's measurements reproduce, but the CUDA lead is smaller on H100 (T1, T3).**
   The median CUDA lead falls with field width on both GPUs, and on the same configurations
   it's 22–24% smaller on H100 at 32–128 bits and 13% smaller at 256 bits.
2. **Most of the lead comes from host-side launch cost, not from Triton's generated kernels (T4).**
   Both backends issue the same kernel launches and host synchronizations. On the Triton side
   each launch costs 10–21 µs of host time, against 3–4 µs from the CUDA executable. At 32
   bits, r=20, the GPU is idle for 90% of Triton's wall time.
   - Kernel-only, CUDA leads by 1.36× at 32 bits (r=20), against 4.03× wall-clock.
   - At 256 bits (r=20), kernel-only it leads by 1.03×, against 1.51× wall-clock.
3. **Triton's kernel gap and extra registers come from its block-reduction idiom (T5).**
   The reshape/split tree in `_store_eval_partial` makes every thread redo the whole 128-lane
   add-mod reduction. Replacing it with `tl.reduce` (`tools/treduce.py`) gives bit-identical
   results.
   - The eval kernel gets 2.2–2.7× faster at 32 bits and 1.1× at 256 bits.
   - The kernel-only CUDA lead drops to 0.92–1.05× (medians by width and table size).
   - Triton's kernels become faster than CUDA's in 24/25 configurations at 32 bits (r=20) and
     18/24 at 256 bits (r=20).
   - The full 800-configuration matrix with `tl.reduce` and the overflow fix validates every
     configuration that fits in memory (758/792). Its median wall-clock CUDA lead at 256 bits is
     1.24× (original 1.33×), with 43 Triton wins in total (original 30).
4. **Register pressure and occupancy explain little (T6).**
   - At 32 bits, dropping CUDA to Triton's occupancy (16 → 10 blocks/SM) costs CUDA at most
     1.25×. The actual eval-kernel gap there is 1.76–5.93×.
   - At 256 bits, occupancy accounts for all of the gap in `zk_vanilla_permcheck_hp` and part
     of it in `zk_spartan_2`.
   - For `zk_witness_id_point_1` the gap persists at equal occupancy. `zk_complete_add_3` runs
     faster in Triton at lower occupancy.
5. **The paper's skips and mismatches have concrete causes (T1, T7, section 5).**
   - An int32 index overflow in the harness's table-encode kernel accounts for every r=28 case
     with more than 2^31 table elements: 5 crashes, 2 wrong checksums, and 1 checksum that is
     correct only by coincidence.
   - Compile-stage failures account for the rest of the non-capacity skips. None come from
     register pressure.
6. **Both backends pass a CPU-reference check (section 4).**
   This covers every workload against the canonical polynomials in `src/zkduel/workloads.py`.

## 2. Reproduction (T1–T3)

- Validated configurations:
  - H100: 751 of 800.
  - A100: 744 (the paper says 746, but 2 of those rows have `checksum_match=false`).
  - 744 are validated on both GPUs, and 7 more only on H100 (section 5).
- Median CUDA lead by width (32 / 64 / 128 / 256 bits):
  - H100: 3.35× / 2.53× / 1.88× / 1.33×.
  - A100: 4.39× / 3.58× / 2.58× / 1.60×. The paper reports 4.40 / 3.58 / 2.58 / 1.60, with
    the 2 mismatched rows included.
- Triton wins (lead < 1): 30 on H100 (7 at 128 bits, 23 at 256) against 22 on A100 (5 + 17).
  The best ratio is 0.643 against 0.640. At 256 bits, 33 configurations are within 1.1×,
  against 26 on A100.
- Table 4 (256-bit, r=24): the family pattern repeats.
  - Complete addition: 1.03× (A100 1.01×).
  - Spartan: 1.55× (A100 1.79×).
  - All matched: 1.15× (A100 1.17×).
- On the same configurations, Triton is 1.44–1.55× faster on H100 and CUDA 1.16–1.25×.
  - The kernel-only leads on an A100 (sm_80, PCIe) and the H100 agree within about 6%
    (`../a100/followups/COMPARISON.md`). So the wall-clock difference is mostly host, not GPU.
  - Host cost is not simply faster on this machine. Triton's per-launch host cost is 17 µs on the
    A100 PCIe host and 21 µs here (32-bit, r=20). The paper's A100 host was not measured.

## 3. Why CUDA leads

**Host cost (T4).**
- The field sweep launches one eval kernel per interpolation point. Each is followed by a
  log-depth tree of reduction launches and a synchronizing device-to-host copy.
- Both backends issue identical numbers of launches: 313 to 1303 per SumCheck run across
  the 25 workloads at r=20 and r=24.
- Host time per launch dominates at small and medium tables. It is roughly constant across
  field widths, while GPU work grows with the limb count. That is why the wall-clock lead
  narrows with width and with table size, which is the trend the paper attributes to register
  pressure.
- Kernel-only leads:
  - 32 bits: 1.36× (r=20), 2.22× (r=24).
  - 256 bits: 1.03× (r=20), 1.13× (r=24).
  - At 256 bits, r=24, Triton's kernels are faster than CUDA's in 4 of the 12 complete-addition
    workloads (`zk_complete_add_3` to `_6`, 0.63–0.78×). Two are at parity (0.96–0.99×) and
    six trail (1.11–1.24×).

**Reduction idiom (T5).**
- The Triton 3.7 TTGIR for `_round_eval_specialized_kernel` converts the 128-element partial
  sums to layouts with `sizePerThread=[64, 2]`, `[32, 2]`, and so on.
- Each thread therefore holds the whole block and performs all 127 modular additions itself.
  CUDA's shared-memory tree needs about one per thread.
- This per-element cost is independent of the expression, so it weighs most on short
  expressions (Spartan) and at narrow widths.
- With `tl.reduce`, Triton's median eval-kernel registers at 32 bits fall from 46 to 32 (CUDA:
  28), and the kernel gap closes.
- At 256 bits the register count barely changes (148 → 146; CUDA: 127), yet the kernel-only
  lead still reaches 0.92–1.01×.

**Occupancy (T6).** A CUDA variant pads each eval block's shared memory to cap resident blocks
per SM (`patches/cuda_occupancy_knob.patch`), leaving the code and registers unchanged.
- At 32 bits, at Triton's occupancy, CUDA's eval kernels slow by 1.00–1.25×. The gap they
  would need to explain is 1.76–5.93×.
- The largest case, `zk_spartan_2` at 32 bits, r=24 (5.93×), is also the one where
  `tl.reduce` gives the biggest speedup: its eval kernel drops from 3.44 ms to 0.58 ms, 5.9×
  (`diag/baseline_all.jsonl` vs `diag/treduce_all.jsonl`).

**Full-matrix sweep with `tl.reduce` (T1, T2, T8, T9).**
- Setup: the 800-configuration sweep reran through the same harness, settings and CUDA binary,
  with Triton's block reduction replaced by `tl.reduce` and the int64-offset fix in place. It
  covers 792 configurations; jellyfish zerocheck at 256 bits was not run.
- Correctness: 758 validated, 0 checksum mismatches, 0 crashes, 0 timeouts. All 34 skips are
  out-of-memory cases with a footprint over 80 GB.
- Wall-clock medians: 3.07× / 2.83× / 1.96× / 1.24× at 32/64/128/256 bits, with 43 Triton wins
  (29 at 256 bits).
- Table 4 at 256 bits, r=24: complete addition 0.98×, incomplete addition 1.01×, all matched
  1.09×.
- Paired with the original run (T8):
  - Triton's wall time falls on large tables at every width. At r=28 it's 0.46× the original at
    32 bits, 0.71× at 64, 0.80× at 128 and 0.87× at 256.
  - At 256 bits it falls at every table size (0.83–0.94×).
  - CUDA, the same binary in both runs, stays at 0.98–1.01×.
- On small tables at 64/128 bits (r ≤ 20) the `tl.reduce` run is 6–15% slower wall-clock. A
  controlled `nsys` re-measurement (T9, r=16) shows the variant's GPU time is lower in all six
  cases checked (eval kernel −7% to −28%). Its wall time differs by −2% to +7%, all of it host
  time.
- So the small-table difference is host-side: run-to-run launch-path variance, plus possibly
  per-launch overhead of the variant. It is not the reduction kernel; the cause isn't pinned
  down further.

## 4. Correctness

**Checks now covering every workload.**
- `tools/validate_vs_registry.py` computes reference checksums from the canonical expression
  registry with plain modular arithmetic. The paper's CPU checker is a transliteration of the
  kernels and covers 14 of 32 workloads at r=2–3. This one runs at r=3 and r=10 (multiple
  blocks) with fixed and SHA3 challenges.
- Triton 3.7 in the sweep's mode: 531/531 rows match, covering 32/32 workloads at 32, 64 and
  128 bits and 31/32 at 256 bits (jellyfish zerocheck at 256 bits does not compile in time).
- CUDA, fixed-harness binary (`build/field_sumcheck_cuda_fixed`, the paper's sweep): 381/381, the
  same cases as Triton, plus jellyfish zerocheck at 256 bits (3/3)
  (`tools/validate_cuda_vs_registry.py`; `validation/cuda_fixed_vs_registry.jsonl`,
  `validation/cuda_jellyfish256_vs_registry.jsonl`). The first binary was checked on 291 rows,
  before the 128- and 256-bit references existed (`validation/cuda_vs_registry.jsonl`).
- `tl.reduce` variant: 36/36.
- Earlier, Triton 3.3 matched 681 rows over both layouts and both point modes.

**Encode-kernel overflow (T7; fixed in the released source).**
- `_encode_tables_kernel` computed `pid * BLOCK_SIZE` in int32. At r=28, every workload with
  9 or more tables (more than 2^31 elements) accessed memory before the table tensor and left
  the elements past 2^31 unencoded.
- The outcome depended on what lay below the tensor:
  - illegal memory access: 5 cases, logged on H100;
  - wrong checksum: `zk_vanilla_zerocheck_hp` at 32 and 64 bits on both GPUs;
  - correct checksum: `zk_vanilla_permcheck_hp` at 64 bits.
- The last one is a coincidence. Its only unencoded tables (N1, N2, N3) occur only together,
  in one term. An unencoded value reads as x·R⁻¹, so the term picks up R⁻³, and in Goldilocks
  R³ = 2^192 ≡ 1 (mod p). This row passed the Triton-vs-CUDA gate while computing on
  corrupted input.
- With the fix, all 8 in-memory cases produce checksums that agree with CUDA.
- The CUDA side had a related wrap: `blockIdx.x * blockDim.x` is uint32, and it wraps for
  jellyfish zerocheck at 32 bits, r=28.

**Montgomery multiplication.**
- The CIOS loop shared by both backends drops the top carry bit (`uvn & mask32`,
  `t[L] = (uint32_t)uvn`).
- It is correct for the four configured primes (0 wrong in 200,000 trials each). It is wrong
  for moduli near 2^(32L): 8/200,000 for 2^64−59 and 12/200,000 for 2^128−159, with inputs
  stressed near the modulus.
- Because both backends share the bug, the cross-backend check would not catch it. Adding a
  field therefore needs a modulus bound, or the kernels need the extra carry word.
- `src/zkduel/fields.py` enforces the bound: constructing a `FieldSpec` with an unsafe modulus
  raises `UnsafeModulusError` (`tests/test_field_guard.py`).

## 5. Skip accounting

The paper splits the A100 skips into 42 that exceed capacity and 12 non-capacity failures.
That split does not match the measured causes.

| Cause | A100 | H100 | Evidence |
|---|---:|---:|---|
| Out of memory (footprint > 80 GB) | 36 | 36 | `torch.OutOfMemoryError` in the H100 logs (`sweep/skips_h100.md`) |
| Encode overflow: crash | 5 | 5 | `illegal memory access` in the H100 logs; fixed (T7) |
| Encode overflow: wrong checksum | 2 | 2 | fixed (T7) |
| jellyfish zerocheck at 256 bits (r=14–24): Triton compile too slow | 6 | 6 (timeouts) | see compile times below |
| First-compile failures that pass with a warm cache | 7 | 0 | these 7 validate on H100 |

The 7 all failed on the first case of their (workload, width), where Triton compiled the
kernels inside the case:
- `zk_complete_add_5`, 256-bit, r=14
- `zk_complete_add_6`, 256-bit, r=14 and r=16
- `zk_complete_add_12`, 256-bit, r=14
- `zk_vanilla_permcheck_hp`, 256-bit, r=14
- `zk_jellyfish_permcheck_hp`, 256-bit, r=14
- `zk_jellyfish_zerocheck_hp`, 128-bit, r=14

The A100 logs are gone, so the exact error for these is unknown, but on H100 every one of them
runs once its kernels are cached.

**jellyfish zerocheck compile times** (Triton 3.7 on this host, one kernel per process, with
the machine heavily loaded by other compiles; `logs/warm_jellyfish.log`):
- 128 bits: 26–42 minutes per eval kernel for points 2–7 (points 0–1 were already cached).
- 256 bits: 1.8 h and 1.9 h for points 0–1; 4.6, 5.1, 5.6, 6.1 and 6.7 h for points 2–6.
- Point 7 was still compiling after 6.8 h when the warm-up deadline stopped it.

So on H100 the jellyfish zerocheck skips at 256 bits are compile time, not a compiler error:
the kernels that finished took 1.8–6.7 hours each, and one was still compiling after 6.8 hours.
In the A100 run, which had no timeout, those cases exited with an error (`returncode=1`) rather
than hanging; the error itself was not recorded.

**Why jellyfish compiles slowly (T12, T17).**
- **The pass.** One pass, `TritonGPUCoalesce`, takes 97.0% / 99.0% / 99.8% of the TTGIR stage at
  32/64/128 bits. The stage and pass times come from the same compile (`diag/compile_passes.jsonl`,
  `logs/mlir_passes_*.txt`).
- **The mechanism.** For every load and store, `buildCoalescedEncoding`
  (`lib/Dialect/TritonGPU/Transforms/CoalesceUtils.cpp`) calls `mlir::getSlice(op)` to find the
  memory ops that share its shape and order.
  - `getSlice` (`mlir/lib/Analysis/SliceAnalysis.cpp`) is a fixpoint loop. It recomputes the full
    backward and forward slice of every op it adds.
  - The field-sweep kernels share one index computation and feed one expression, so every slice is
    the whole kernel.
  - The pass is therefore O(M·N²): M memory ops, N ops.
- **Synthetic kernel (T17, `tools/coalesce_scaling.py`).** Log-log slope 1.06 in M and 2.26 in N on
  3.7.0; 1.07 and 2.36 on 3.8.0.
- **Jellyfish.**
  - Each limb doubling roughly doubles M (2 × 22 × L loads) and N (6.6k / 13.1k / 27.3k TTIR ops).
  - Pass time grows 11–12× per doubling: 11 s / 123 s / 1516 s on 3.7.0.
  - Extrapolated to 8 limbs, that is about 5 h per kernel, matching the times above.
- **Persistence.** 3.8.0 has the same scaling and is 13–20% faster. `CoalesceUtils.cpp` is identical
  in 3.7.0, 3.8.0 and Triton main, and `getSlice` is identical in LLVM main.
- **Remedies.**
  - Compiler side: group memory ops once per connected component, which is linear in N.
  - Kernel side: fewer memory ops or ops per kernel.
    - The naive version, one 2-D load plus `tl.split` per table (`tools/vecload.py`, bit-identical
      results), is not a fix.
    - It raised `zk_spartan_2` 32-bit registers from 46 to 255 and moved compile time into LLVM
      lowering.
    - On the 32-bit jellyfish kernel it halves M, and Coalesce falls from 11.1 s to 5.9 s, as
      O(M·N²) predicts.
    - LLVM lowering then took 258 s and `ptxas` more than 6 min before it was stopped
      (`logs/mlir_passes_vecload_3.7.0_32.txt`).

## 6. Methodology notes and further measurements

- **Correctness gating.**
  - The comparison drivers compare Triton against CUDA. They write a row for every
    configuration; a row whose checksums disagree gets no speedup, and the summary tools exclude
    it (`docs/interpreting_results.md`).
  - The CPU checker `cuda/check/check_field_sumcheck.py` covers 14 of 32 workloads at r=2–3, and
    its checksum is over the round evaluations, not the final table. The registry checks in
    section 4 cover every workload.
- **Counts (A100 sweep, T1).**
  - 744 configurations validate. 746 ran on both backends; 2 of them have mismatched checksums
    (T7).
  - The 22 configurations where Triton is faster are 17 at 256 bits and 5 at 128 bits.
- **Timing settings.** The sweeps use a fixed challenge (r=1) and whole-run timing, not SHA3
  challenges.
- **Mechanism.** Most of the CUDA lead is host launch cost, and most of the remaining kernel gap
  is the block-reduction idiom (T4–T6). Register pressure is a secondary factor in some 256-bit
  kernels.
- **Interpolation.** Each interpolation point is a separate eval kernel that recomputes d. The
  kernels do not form d once and reach the points by successive additions.
- **Limb-major layout (T15).** 1–5% faster at 256 bits with specialized points, neutral or
  slightly slower with generic points, and identical at 32 bits: a minor optimization.
- **Specialized interpolation (T15).** At 256 bits it makes eval kernels 1.55–1.59× faster in
  Triton and 1.65–1.75× in CUDA; at 32 bits it gives about 1.04×.
  - It does not uniformly cut registers: Triton's per-kernel maximum rises for
    `zk_complete_add_3` (119 → 154).
  - All sweeps use specialized points (`--point-mode specialized`).
- **CUDA tuning (T16).** Block size, the only parameter, is at or near its best at 128 threads.
  At 32 bits, 256–512 threads trims up to 8% of wall-clock through fewer reduction launches.
- **Modulus bound.** The CIOS routine needs a modulus bound (section 4).

## 7. What a second GPU generation tests

- A100 and H100 have the same per-SM register file (64K), warp limit (64) and CTA limit (32),
  so moving to H100 does not change the mapping from registers to occupancy.
- `ptxas` allocation is also nearly unchanged. For the product-chain workload, CUDA uses 24/92
  registers (32/256-bit, generic points) on both sm_80 and sm_90. Other kernels shift by up to
  about 12 registers; for `zk_complete_add_3` at 256 bits, the shift from 138 to 128 moves CUDA
  from 3 to 4 blocks per SM.
- What the H100 run does show:
  - the width trend reproduces (T1, T3);
  - the kernel-only comparison, which depends on the architecture and not on the host CPU,
    puts the backends near parity at 256 bits (T4);
  - the occupancy ablation (T6) tests the mechanism directly on one GPU;
  - the `tl.reduce` ablation (T5) removes most of the remaining kernel gap.
- For occupancy thresholds that actually differ, an Ada-class GPU (48 warps/SM) would be the
  informative second platform.

## 8. Caveats

- Host-cost numbers depend on the host (CPU, VM, driver); they differ between the machines measured.
- Kernel-only times are sums of nsys kernel durations per SumCheck run; host time is wall
  minus kernel time, i.e. time the GPU is idle.
- The occupancy ablation covers 5 workloads; the `tl.reduce` and kernel-only comparisons cover
  all 25 at r=20 and r=24.
- `build/field_sumcheck_cuda`, used by the first sweep (`sweep/`), was built from the source
  before the int64-index fix. The r=28 reruns used `build/field_sumcheck_cuda_int64_r28`, and the
  fixed-harness sweep `build/field_sumcheck_cuda_fixed` (built from the released source).
- The compile times above were measured with many compiles running concurrently.
- Run-to-run noise (T18).
  - Kernel-only medians reproduce within 1%.
  - Wall-clock medians usually reproduce within 5%, but a few runs deviate by up to 48% (Triton) or
    32% (CUDA). Pinning to a vCPU does not remove this.
  - Compare single configurations on kernel time. Use wall-clock only in aggregate.
